Why chlorine destroys RO membranes

Municipal water is chlorinated by design, and many plants chlorinate their own raw water storage to control bacteria. That chlorine does a useful job right up until the point it reaches the membrane.

Thin-film composite membranes are built on a polyamide layer, and polyamide is oxidised by free chlorine. The damage is cumulative and permanent. A membrane exposed to chlorine keeps passing water — often at a higher flow than before, which fools operators into thinking nothing is wrong — but salt rejection falls away and never comes back. Product TDS rises and stays risen.

The industry measures this exposure in ppm-hours. It does not take a large dose; it takes time at a small dose. This is why continuous SMBS dosing matters more than most operators expect.

How SMBS works

SMBS is a reducing agent. In water it forms sodium bisulphite, which reacts with free chlorine and converts it to harmless chloride:

  • Fast reaction — neutralises free chlorine within seconds of mixing, ahead of the membrane.
  • Simple end product — the reaction leaves chloride and sulphate in solution — both handled normally by the RO.
  • Measurable — residual chlorine downstream should read zero; that is your daily confirmation the dosing is working.

SMBS is normally dosed after the activated carbon filter as a second line of defence, because carbon capacity for chlorine falls off as the media exhausts — often without warning.

Common questions

How much SMBS do I need per ppm of chlorine?

Roughly 3 mg of SMBS neutralises 1 mg of free chlorine. Most plants dose slightly above the calculated figure to hold a small margin against variation in the incoming supply.

Where should SMBS be dosed?

After the activated carbon filter and before the cartridge filter. That order gives it enough contact time to react before water reaches the membranes.

Do I still need SMBS if I have a carbon filter?

Yes, in almost every case. Carbon removes chlorine well when fresh, but its capacity falls off gradually and there is no warning when it runs out. SMBS is the backstop that catches what gets past.

How do I check that dosing is working?

Test free chlorine at the membrane inlet daily. It should read zero. Any reading above zero means your dose is short or the solution has lost strength.

How often should I make fresh solution?

Every one to three days. SMBS solution degrades on contact with air. Keeping the dosing tank covered slows it down but does not stop it.

What if chlorine already got through?

Test permeate TDS against the plant's baseline. A permanent rise in salt passage, especially with flow holding or rising, points to oxidation damage — cleaning will not recover it. Send us your figures and we will advise.
