Why RO permeate is corrosive

RO does its job well: it strips out dissolved salts. But the same process removes the natural alkalinity that keeps water chemically stable. Permeate typically comes out mildly acidic and with almost no buffering capacity, which makes it aggressive towards metal.

In practice that shows up as pinhole leaks in mild steel and copper lines, corroded storage tank fittings, blue-green staining on brass and copper, and metallic taste in stored water. Plants often replace the piping and see the same failure repeat two years later, without connecting it to the water chemistry.

The second use is on the feed side. Some borewell water is naturally acidic, and low-pH feed both corrodes the pre-treatment equipment and pushes the RO to run outside its optimum range.

How ZS-RO-106 works

Sodium carbonate dissolves to give a stable alkaline solution that lifts pH and restores buffering:

  • Raises pH predictably — a controlled, gradual lift without the sharp swings caustic soda produces.
  • Restores buffer capacity — carbonate alkalinity resists pH drift, so the water stays stable in storage.
  • Reduces corrosion — less aggressive water means longer life for piping, tanks, fittings and valves.

It is supplied as a powder, dissolved in the dosing tank and injected through a standard metering pump — the same setup you already use for antiscalant.

Common questions

Why does my RO water corrode pipes?

RO removes the alkalinity that normally buffers water, leaving it mildly acidic and aggressive towards metal. It is the low pH and absent buffering, not any impurity, that causes the corrosion.

How much should I dose to reach pH 7?

It depends on your starting pH and flow. A typical permeate at pH 6.0 needs roughly 10–15 ppm. Measure after dosing and adjust — send us your figures and we will calculate the setting.

Can I use caustic soda instead?

Caustic soda works but is harsh to handle and moves pH sharply, so it is easy to overshoot. Sodium carbonate lifts pH gradually and adds buffering capacity, which caustic does not.

Where should it be dosed?

Into the permeate line before storage for corrosion control, or into the feed line when correcting acidic raw water. The two applications need different dose points and rates.

Is it safe for drinking water?

Sodium carbonate is used routinely in drinking water treatment at these levels. Use food-grade material for potable applications and confirm the grade when ordering.

The dosing pump runs but pH is not changing. Why?

Almost always undissolved powder blocking the pump suction, or a solution that has settled out in the tank. Drain, mix a fresh batch with full dissolution, and stir before each shift.
