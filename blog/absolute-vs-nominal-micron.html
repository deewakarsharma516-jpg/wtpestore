Absolute vs nominal micron rating — the real difference

Aqua Filtration System, Faridabad · 9+ years in water treatment · Updated September 2026

Short answer: Nominal 5 micron means roughly 85 to 90 percent of particles are retained. Absolute 5 micron means about 99.9 percent. For a general RO plant nominal is sufficient and much cheaper. Absolute is needed where a guarantee is required — pharma, food, or where the membrane is particularly expensive.

The difference in plain terms

The pores inside a filter are not all identical — they cannot be.

  • Nominal: "most pores are 5 micron, some are a little larger" — so a few particles get through
  • Absolute: "no pore is larger than 5 micron" — so almost nothing gets through

Is 85 to 90 percent enough?

For an RO plant, yes. The reasons:

  • The 10 to 15 percent that pass are particles close to 5 micron, not large ones
  • The RO membrane itself is extremely fine and retains them
  • The real threat is coarse particles, and a nominal filter stops those
So: buying absolute filters for a general RO plant is money wasted. That money is better spent on good pre-treatment — a bag filter or sand filter.

When absolute is genuinely required

1. Pharma and food industry

These involve validation and audit — you have to produce documentation of what the filter retains. A nominal filter carries no such guarantee.

2. Very expensive membranes

An 8040 or seawater membrane costs a great deal. Spending ₹1,000 to ₹2,000 on an absolute filter there is effectively insurance.

3. Final polishing

0.45 or 0.22 micron absolute retains bacteria. This is used in packaged water and laboratory work.

0.22 and 0.45 micron — sterile filtration

0.45Most bacteriaLaboratory, testing
0.22Effectively all bacteriaPharma, sterile applications

These are almost always absolute, because a nominal rating would be meaningless — 10 percent of bacteria passing is still a failure.

Note: a 0.22 micron filter is never fitted before RO — it would choke immediately. It goes at the final stage, after RO, where the water is already clean.

How to tell whether a filter is absolute

  • The packaging states it clearly — "absolute" or "99.9% at X micron"
  • It is usually of pleated construction
  • The price is considerably higher
  • A certificate or test report often accompanies it

If the packaging says only "5 micron" and nothing more, it is nominal.

A selling practice to watch for

In some places an absolute price is charged for a nominal filter. To protect yourself:

  • Ask for the test report — it accompanies genuine absolute filters
  • Check the packaging — the rating should be clearly printed
  • Buy only from a supplier you trust

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between nominal and absolute micron?

Nominal means roughly eighty five to ninety percent of particles are retained, while absolute means about ninety nine point nine percent. In an absolute filter no pore is larger than the rated size, which is why it costs considerably more.

Does an RO plant need absolute filters?

Generally no. Nominal five micron is adequate for RO, because the few particles that pass are very small and the membrane itself retains them. That money is better spent on improving pre-treatment.

When should absolute filters be used?

In pharma and food industry where validation and audit apply, or where the membrane is particularly expensive such as an 8040 or seawater element. Sterile filtration uses zero point two two micron absolute.

How can I tell whether a filter is absolute?

The packaging states it clearly, either as absolute or as ninety nine point nine percent at a given micron, and a test report usually accompanies it. If it says only five micron with nothing further, it is nominal.

Can a 0.22 micron filter be fitted before RO?

No, it would choke immediately. It belongs at the final stage after RO, where the water is already clean. Before RO, five micron is the appropriate rating.
