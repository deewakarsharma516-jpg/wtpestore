Boiler oxygen scavenger — why it is essential

Aqua Filtration System, Faridabad · 9+ years in water treatment · Updated September 2026

Short answer: Dissolved oxygen in boiler feed water causes pitting corrosion — small deep pits that penetrate tube walls far faster than general corrosion. An oxygen scavenger chemically removes that dissolved oxygen. Without it, boiler tubes and the condensate system corrode from the inside.

See our ZeroScale boiler oxygen scavenger.

Why dissolved oxygen is so damaging

Oxygen dissolved in water attacks steel at specific points rather than uniformly. The result is pitting — narrow, deep holes rather than general thinning.

  • A pit can penetrate a tube wall while the surrounding metal is still sound
  • Failure is sudden rather than gradual
  • Inspection can miss pits because overall wall thickness looks acceptable
This is why oxygen control is treated so seriously in boiler operation. A tube failure means an unplanned shutdown, and in a pressure system it is a safety matter, not just a maintenance one.

Where oxygen enters

  • Make-up water — fresh water carries dissolved oxygen
  • Condensate return — air leaks into the system where it runs below atmospheric pressure
  • Pump glands and fittings on the suction side
  • Feed tank exposed to air

The more make-up water a system takes, the more oxygen enters — which is one reason condensate recovery is worthwhile.

Two stages of oxygen removal

1. Mechanical — deaeration

Heating the feed water reduces the solubility of oxygen, so much of it is driven off. A deaerator or a heated feed tank removes the bulk of the oxygen.

2. Chemical — oxygen scavenger

Mechanical deaeration never removes it all. The scavenger deals with the remainder, and provides protection when the deaerator is not performing perfectly.

Both are needed. Chemical dosing alone on cold make-up water means a very high chemical consumption; deaeration alone leaves residual oxygen.

Where to dose

Into the feed tank or deaerator storage section, so the chemical has contact time before the water reaches the boiler.

Dosing directly at the boiler inlet gives too little reaction time, and the oxygen will already have passed through the feed line.

Dosing considerations

  • Dose is based on the dissolved oxygen present plus a residual, so it depends heavily on feed water temperature
  • Cold make-up water needs far more chemical than deaerated hot water
  • Maintain a measurable residual in the boiler water — that is the confirmation the dose is adequate
  • Use a dosing pump rated for the boiler feed line pressure — see our pump selection guide
Raising feed water temperature reduces chemical consumption significantly, because less oxygen remains dissolved. Where a boiler runs on cold make-up, improving the feed heating often pays for itself in chemical savings alone.

Signs that oxygen control is inadequate

  • Iron levels rising in the boiler water or condensate
  • Pitting found during inspection
  • Reddish deposits in the feed system
  • Repeated tube failures at similar locations
  • No measurable scavenger residual in the boiler water

Safety

  • Handle with gloves and eye protection
  • Store away from other chemicals and label clearly
  • Keep the dosing tank covered
  • Follow the product safety data sheet

Frequently asked questions

Why is dissolved oxygen so damaging in a boiler?

It causes pitting corrosion, which produces narrow deep holes rather than general thinning. A pit can penetrate a tube wall while the surrounding metal still looks sound, so failure is sudden and inspection can miss it.

Where does oxygen enter a boiler system?

Mainly through make-up water, which carries dissolved oxygen, and through air leaking into the condensate return where it runs below atmospheric pressure. Pump glands and open feed tanks also admit air.

Is a deaerator enough on its own?

No. Mechanical deaeration removes the bulk of the oxygen but never all of it, and it may not perform perfectly at all times. A chemical scavenger deals with the remainder and provides protection when the deaerator underperforms.

Where should the oxygen scavenger be dosed?

Into the feed tank or deaerator storage section, so there is contact time before the water reaches the boiler. Dosing at the boiler inlet gives too little reaction time.

How do I know the dose is adequate?

By maintaining a measurable scavenger residual in the boiler water. Rising iron levels in boiler water or condensate indicate that oxygen control is inadequate.
