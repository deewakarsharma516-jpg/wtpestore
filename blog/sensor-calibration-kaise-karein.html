How to calibrate sensors on site — step by step

Aqua Filtration System, Faridabad · 9+ years in water treatment · Updated September 2026

Short answer: Calibration means showing the sensor a known solution and telling it "this is 7" or "this is 1,413 µS/cm", so that it reads correctly afterwards. It should be done monthly, and it can be done on site with nothing more than buffer solution and ten minutes.

Without calibration even the most expensive sensor reads incorrectly — and decisions based on wrong readings can be very costly.

Why calibration is necessary

Sensors drift over time. Something that read 7.0 yesterday may read 7.3 today, without anyone noticing.

The real damage is that you then make decisions on a wrong reading — overdosing chemical, or replacing a membrane that was actually fine.

A second benefit: calibration also tells you the condition of the sensor. If the controller will not accept the calibration, or a larger adjustment is needed each time, the sensor has weakened and should be replaced.

pH sensor — two-point calibration

  1. Prepare: buffer 7 and buffer 4 (or 9.2), clean water, tissue
  2. Remove the sensor, rinse with clean water and blot gently — do not rub
  3. Place in buffer 7 and allow one to two minutes to settle
  4. Enter calibration mode and set 7.00
  5. Rinse, then place in buffer 4 (or 9.2)
  6. Set that value once settled
  7. Refit the sensor in the line

Which second buffer: whichever side you work on. Buffer 4 if you dose acid, buffer 9.2 if you dose alkali.

Conductivity and TDS calibration

  1. Take a standard solution, usually 1,413 µS/cm (or 1,000 ppm TDS)
  2. Rinse and dry the sensor
  3. Immerse in the standard and let it settle
  4. Set that value on the controller
The most common mistake: putting a wet sensor straight into the standard. Water on the sensor dilutes the standard and the calibration is wrong. Rinse the sensor once in the same standard first, then calibrate in fresh standard.

ORP calibration

ORP sensors need calibrating less often. Place in an ORP standard solution, typically 470 mV, and set that value.

If the reading differs from the standard by more than 30 to 40 mV, clean the platinum tip — gently, never scrubbed.

DO calibration — in air

This is the simplest of all:

  1. Remove the sensor and blot it gently
  2. Leave it in air for 5 to 10 minutes (in humid air is better — near a damp cloth)
  3. Set "100% saturation" or "air calibration" on the controller

Because the oxygen content of open air is constant, it serves as a reliable reference.

About buffer solutions

  • Check the expiry date — old buffer gives a wrong calibration
  • Do not use straight from the bottle — pour a little into a separate cup. Dipping the sensor into the bottle spoils the whole bottle
  • Discard used buffer — never reuse it
  • Keep it closed — open buffer degrades in air, buffer 9.2 particularly
  • Store cool, away from sunlight

Five common mistakes

  1. Rubbing the sensor — the glass membrane is thin, and a scratch ruins the sensor
  2. Rushing the calibration — allow one to two minutes for the reading to settle
  3. Expired buffer — gives a wrong value
  4. One-point calibration only — buffer 7 alone is incomplete; the second point is essential
  5. Not recording the calibration — note the date and values each time, so you can see the trend

When to replace instead

Calibration itself gives you the answer:

  • The controller will not accept the calibration at all
  • The adjustment required grows month on month
  • It reads around 6 or 7 even in buffer 4
  • Readings take two to three minutes to settle

Frequently asked questions

How often should sensors be calibrated?

pH and ORP sensors monthly, conductivity every three months, and turbidity every three to six months. In difficult water such as an ETP more frequent calibration may be needed.

Which buffers are needed for pH calibration?

Buffer seven always, together with either buffer four or buffer nine point two. Which second buffer you use depends on whether you work on the acid side or the alkaline side.

How does calibration reveal the condition of a sensor?

If the controller will not accept the calibration, or a larger adjustment is needed each time, the sensor has weakened. That is why the values should be recorded every time, so the drift can be tracked.

Can buffer solution be reused?

No. Used buffer should be discarded because it has been diluted by water from the sensor. Dipping the sensor directly into the bottle is also wrong; pour a little into a separate cup instead.

Is single-point calibration acceptable?

Single-point calibration is incomplete. It sets the zero point but does not check the slope, meaning the sensitivity of the sensor. Two-point calibration should always be done.
