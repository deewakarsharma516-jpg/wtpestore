Condensate line corrosion — causes and prevention

Aqua Filtration System, Faridabad · 9+ years in water treatment · Updated September 2026

Short answer: Condensate corrodes for two reasons. Carbon dioxide carried over with the steam dissolves in the condensate and forms carbonic acid, which lowers the pH and attacks the pipework. Oxygen leaking into the system adds pitting corrosion on top of that.

See our ZeroScale condensate corrosion inhibitor.

Why condensate is aggressive

Condensate is essentially distilled water — pure, with almost no dissolved salts. That sounds ideal, but pure water is actually aggressive towards metal, and it has very little buffering capacity, so a small amount of acid drops the pH sharply.

The carbon dioxide route

  1. Bicarbonate alkalinity in the boiler feed water breaks down at boiler temperature
  2. This releases carbon dioxide, which leaves with the steam
  3. When the steam condenses, the CO₂ dissolves into the condensate
  4. It forms carbonic acid, lowering the pH
  5. The acidic condensate attacks the pipework
The characteristic pattern is general thinning of the pipe, often grooved along the bottom where condensate runs. Iron levels in the condensate rise, and eventually pinholes appear.

The oxygen route

Condensate lines often run below atmospheric pressure in parts of the system, so air is drawn in through:

  • Threaded joints and flanges
  • Pump glands
  • Vented receivers and open tanks
  • Any leak point

This oxygen causes pitting — the same mechanism described in our oxygen scavenger guide.

Why it matters commercially

  • Condensate is valuable — it is hot, treated and essentially pure, so returning it saves both fuel and treatment chemical
  • Corroded lines force you to dump condensate rather than return it, because the iron would go into the boiler
  • Iron entering the boiler causes deposits on tubes
  • Pipe replacement and leak repairs are expensive and disruptive
The economics are clear: every litre of condensate not returned has to be replaced with cold treated make-up water, which costs fuel to heat and chemical to treat, and brings fresh oxygen into the system. Protecting the condensate system pays back through condensate recovery.

Chemical treatment

Neutralising amines

These volatile chemicals travel with the steam, condense along with it, and neutralise the carbonic acid, raising the condensate pH into a protective range.

Filming amines

These form a thin protective film on the metal surface, providing a physical barrier against both CO₂ and oxygen.

Reducing the CO₂ at source

Chemical treatment addresses the symptom. The root cause is bicarbonate alkalinity in the feed water, so reducing that reduces the CO₂ produced:

  • Dealkalisation of the make-up water
  • RO treatment of make-up, which removes bicarbonate along with other salts
  • Maximising condensate return, so less fresh make-up is needed in the first place

On systems with high make-up and high feed alkalinity, treating the make-up often costs less than continuously dosing amine.

Fixing air ingress

  • Repair leaking joints, particularly on the vacuum side
  • Check and maintain pump glands
  • Ensure receivers are properly sealed where possible
  • Check vents are correctly sized — oversized vents admit air
  • Maintain steam traps, since failed traps disrupt the whole system

Condensate iron is the key indicator. It tells you directly whether metal is being lost, which pH alone does not.

A note on conductivity monitoring

Condensate conductivity also serves as a contamination alarm. A sudden rise indicates a process leak into the condensate — for example a heat exchanger failure. If contaminated condensate returns to the boiler, the consequences are considerably worse than corrosion.

A conductivity controller with relay (₹5,880) can divert contaminated condensate to drain automatically. See our conductivity guide.

Frequently asked questions

Why do condensate lines corrode?

Carbon dioxide carried over with the steam dissolves in the condensate and forms carbonic acid, lowering the pH and attacking the pipework. Oxygen leaking into the system adds pitting corrosion on top of that.

Where does the carbon dioxide come from?

From bicarbonate alkalinity in the boiler feed water, which breaks down at boiler temperature and releases carbon dioxide. That leaves with the steam and dissolves into the condensate when it condenses.

Why does condensate corrosion matter commercially?

Condensate is hot, treated and essentially pure, so returning it saves fuel and chemical. Corroded lines force you to dump it instead, and every litre not returned has to be replaced with cold make-up that costs fuel and treatment and brings in fresh oxygen.

What is the key indicator to monitor?

Condensate iron. It tells you directly whether metal is being lost, which pH alone does not. Condensate pH should be checked daily and iron weekly.

Can the carbon dioxide be reduced at source?

Yes, by reducing bicarbonate alkalinity in the make-up water through dealkalisation or RO treatment, and by maximising condensate return so less fresh make-up is needed. On high make-up systems this often costs less than continuous amine dosing.
