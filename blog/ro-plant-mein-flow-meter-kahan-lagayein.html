Where to fit flow meters on an RO plant

Aqua Filtration System, Faridabad · 9+ years in water treatment · Updated September 2026

Short answer: Every RO plant needs at least two flow meters — one on permeate and one on reject. Together they give you recovery, which is the single best indicator of plant health. On larger plants a third meter on the feed line is worthwhile.

Many plants have only one meter fitted, and that is the mistake — with one meter you cannot diagnose anything.

Three locations, three purposes

1. Permeate line — the essential one

This tells you how much water the plant is producing. If it falls, the membrane is fouling, the pressure has dropped, or the pre-filter is choked.

What to watch: it should read close to the rated capacity of the plant. A drop of more than 20 percent needs investigation.

2. Reject line — equally essential

This one protects the membrane. If reject flow falls too low, scale begins to form on the membrane and it fails early.

What to watch: reject flow must never go below the manufacturer's stated minimum, however tempting it is to increase recovery.

3. Feed line — on larger plants

Feed equals permeate plus reject. If those three do not add up, there is a leak somewhere or one meter is wrong. Worth fitting on plants above 2,000 LPH.

Calculating recovery — and why it matters

Recovery (%) = (Permeate ÷ Feed) × 100
where Feed = Permeate + Reject

Example: permeate 500 LPH, reject 270 LPH → feed = 770 LPH → recovery = (500 ÷ 770) × 100 = 65 percent

The most common mistake: throttling the reject valve to raise recovery and save water. A few months later the membrane is scaled and has to be replaced — and a new membrane costs many times more than the water that was saved.

Exact positions in the layout

  1. Raw water inlet — optional, fit one if you need a record of borewell or supply usage
  2. After the pre-filter, before the HP pump — the feed meter position. Fitting here also shows when the pre-filter is choking
  3. After the membrane, on the permeate line — the essential meter, placed before the storage tank
  4. On the reject line, before the reject valve — the second essential meter
A practical tip: fit the permeate and reject meters next to each other so the operator can see both at a glance and work out recovery immediately. Meters fitted in separate corners never get looked at.

What to fit alongside

Flow alone does not give the full picture. With these three together you can identify almost any plant problem:

  • Pressure gauges — before the pump and after the membrane. Flow down and pressure up means membrane fouling
  • TDS or conductivity meter — permeate quality. Flow normal but TDS rising means the membrane is at end of life
  • Low pressure switch — protects the pump from dry running

Frequently asked questions

How many flow meters does an RO plant need?

At least two, one on the permeate line and one on the reject line. Together they give you recovery, which is the best single indicator of plant health. On plants above two thousand LPH a third meter on the feed line is also worthwhile.

What recovery should an RO plant run at?

It depends on feed TDS. Below five hundred TDS, seventy to seventy five percent is achievable. Between one and two thousand TDS, sixty to sixty five percent, and above two thousand keep it between fifty and sixty percent. Pushing recovery higher shortens membrane life.

Is a permeate meter alone enough?

No. With only the permeate reading you cannot tell whether a drop in flow is caused by the membrane, the pre-filter or the pump. With a reject meter as well you can calculate recovery and identify the problem immediately.

Is it acceptable to throttle reject to save water?

No. Reject flow is what keeps the membrane clean. Reducing it too far allows scale to form and the membrane fails early. A replacement membrane costs many times more than the water saved.

Where exactly should the permeate meter go?

After the membrane and before the storage tank, so it shows the true plant output. The reject meter goes before the reject valve. Fit both close together so the operator can read them at the same time.
