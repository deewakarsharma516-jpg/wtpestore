When to replace an RO membrane — five clear signs

Aqua Filtration System, Faridabad · 9+ years in water treatment · Updated September 2026

Short answer: A membrane needs replacing when permeate TDS keeps rising even after CIP cleaning. Falling flow on its own usually means fouling, which cleaning can reverse. Rising TDS that cleaning does not fix means the membrane itself has degraded.

Typical membrane life is 2 to 4 years, but that depends almost entirely on how well the pre-treatment and dosing are managed.

Record the baseline first

You cannot judge decline without knowing the starting point. When a membrane is new, record:

  • Permeate flow (LPH)
  • Reject flow (LPH)
  • Feed pressure and permeate pressure
  • Feed TDS and permeate TDS
  • Feed water temperature
Without this baseline, every later reading is guesswork. Write it on a card and keep it at the plant — it takes five minutes and saves a great deal of argument later.

Sign 1: Permeate TDS rising

This is the decisive one. Calculate rejection:

Rejection % = (Feed TDS − Permeate TDS) ÷ Feed TDS × 100
RejectionCondition
96 – 98%New or healthy membrane
92 – 96%Ageing but usable
88 – 92%Nearing end of life
Below 88%Replace

Measure with a TDS or conductivity meter on both feed and permeate — you need both to calculate rejection at all.

Sign 2: Permeate flow falling

A drop of more than 15 percent from the baseline needs investigation. But note that low flow alone does not mean the membrane is finished — read it together with pressure.

Sign 3: Feed pressure rising

If the pump has to work harder to produce the same flow, something is restricting the membrane surface.

Reading the three together

This is what actually tells you the answer:

FlowPressureTDSDiagnosisAction
DownUpNormalScaling or foulingCIP cleaning
DownDownNormalPre-filter choked or pump weakCheck upstream
NormalNormalUpMembrane degradedReplace
UpDownUpMembrane or O-ring damagedInspect, likely replace
DownUpUpHeavy fouling plus damageClean first, then judge
The key distinction: flow down with pressure up is fouling — clean it. TDS up with flow and pressure normal is degradation — replace it. Many membranes are thrown away when a CIP would have restored them.

Sign 4: No improvement after CIP

Carry out a proper CIP cleaning. If flow and rejection return, the membrane was simply fouled. If neither improves meaningfully, the membrane is finished.

See our CIP cleaning guide for the procedure.

Sign 5: Chlorine damage

Chlorine attacks the polyamide layer of a thin film membrane. The damage is permanent and irreversible.

The signature is distinctive — flow increases while TDS rises sharply. The membrane has effectively developed holes, so more water passes but it is not being purified.

Prevention: remove all chlorine before the membrane by dosing SMBS. See our SMBS page.

Monitoring that catches problems early

Fit these and record readings weekly:

  • Rotameters on permeate and reject — see our flow meter placement guide
  • Pressure gauges before and after the membrane
  • TDS meters on feed and permeate

A weekly record of six numbers tells you months in advance that a membrane is declining, so replacement can be planned rather than forced.

Normalising for temperature

One thing that confuses people: membranes produce less water in cold weather. Permeate flow can fall 20 to 30 percent in winter with nothing wrong at all.

So always compare readings at a similar temperature, or you will replace a perfectly good membrane in January.

Planning replacement

  • Replace all elements together in a multi-element vessel — mixing old and new gives poor results
  • Keep a spare if the plant cannot stop
  • Replace the O-rings and brine seal at the same time — these are cheap and a worn seal lets feed water bypass
  • Record the new baseline immediately after fitting

Frequently asked questions

How do I know whether to clean or replace a membrane?

If flow has fallen and pressure has risen but TDS is normal, the membrane is fouled and cleaning should restore it. If TDS has risen while flow and pressure are normal, the membrane has degraded and needs replacing.

What rejection should a healthy membrane give?

A new membrane should give ninety six to ninety eight percent rejection. Below about eighty eight percent it should be replaced. You need TDS readings on both feed and permeate to calculate it.

How long does an RO membrane last?

Typically two to four years, but that depends almost entirely on pre-treatment and dosing. Chlorine, scaling and poor filtration all shorten life considerably.

Why does permeate flow drop in winter?

Membranes produce less water at lower temperature, and flow can fall twenty to thirty percent in cold weather with nothing actually wrong. Always compare readings taken at a similar temperature.

What happens if chlorine reaches the membrane?

It attacks the polyamide layer and the damage is permanent. The signature is flow increasing while TDS rises sharply, because the membrane has effectively developed holes. SMBS dosing prevents this.
