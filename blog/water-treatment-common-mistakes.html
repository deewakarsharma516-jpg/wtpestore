Ten mistakes that cost water treatment plants the most

Aqua Filtration System, Faridabad · 9+ years in water treatment · Updated September 2026

Short answer: Nearly every expensive plant failure we see comes down to one of ten mistakes — and every one of them is preventable for a few thousand rupees. This is a summary of what actually goes wrong, ranked by how often we encounter it.

1. Dosing tank runs empty unnoticed

What happens: antiscalant or SMBS runs out. The plant carries on running with no protection, and by the time anyone notices, the membranes are scaled or chlorine damaged.

Prevention: a level switch at ₹1,050, and a daily glance at the tank level.

This is number one for a reason. It is the most common and among the most expensive.

2. No pressure gauges

What happens: nobody knows when the cartridge is choked, when the media needs backwashing, or whether the pump is worn. Cartridges get changed too late or too early, and faults are diagnosed by guesswork.

Prevention: gauges at ₹315 each. See our placement guide.

3. Pre-treatment sized on output instead of feed flow

What happens: a 750 LPH plant at 65 percent recovery has a feed flow of 1,150 LPH. If the cartridge filter and pumps were sized for 750, everything chokes immediately.

Prevention: size every pre-treatment stage on feed flow. See our design guide.

4. Chlorine reaching the membrane

What happens: municipal feed water contains chlorine. Without dechlorination the membranes degrade continuously, and the damage is permanent.

The signature: permeate flow up and TDS up together.

Prevention: SMBS dosing or carbon filtration, and a DPD test before the membrane. See our SMBS guide.

5. Closing the reject valve to restore output

What happens: output falls, someone closes the reject valve, output appears to recover. But recovery has been raised, the reject stream is more concentrated, and the scaling accelerates. The plant then declines faster.

Prevention: diagnose the actual cause. See our recovery guide.

6. Sensors never calibrated

What happens: a pH or TDS sensor drifts. Decisions are then made on wrong readings — chemical overdosed, or a discharge limit breached without anyone knowing.

Prevention: monthly calibration, taking ten minutes. See our calibration guide.

7. Cleaning the membrane too late

What happens: people wait until output has halved. By then the fouling is firmly attached and CIP recovers only part of the performance.

Prevention: clean at 10 to 15 percent decline, not 50. See our CIP guide.

8. Softener failing unnoticed

What happens: brine stops being drawn, usually because of salt bridging or a blocked brine line. The softener runs but removes nothing, and hard water goes to the boiler or RO for weeks.

Prevention: a daily hardness test with a test kit at ₹656. See our troubleshooting guide.

9. No vacuum breaker on vessels

What happens: a vessel drains, no air can enter, and the vacuum collapses the vessel inward or draws media into the pipework.

Prevention: a vacuum breaker at ₹210. See our vessel guide.

10. No baseline record

What happens: nobody wrote down the readings when the plant was new. So when performance declines, there is nothing to compare against and every judgement becomes an opinion.

Prevention: a notebook and five minutes at commissioning. See our maintenance guide.

The pattern behind all ten

Look at the list and a theme emerges. Almost every one is a monitoring failure rather than an equipment failure:

  • Something stopped working
  • Nothing was measuring it
  • Nobody found out for weeks
  • By then the damage was done

The equipment on most plants is perfectly adequate. What is usually missing is the handful of inexpensive instruments that would have shown the problem while it was still cheap to fix.

Where to start

If your plant has none of this, add it in this order:

  1. Level switches on dosing tanks — biggest risk, lowest cost
  2. Pressure gauges across the cartridge filter
  3. Hardness test kit, if you have a softener
  4. Rotameters on permeate and reject
  5. TDS meter on feed as well as permeate
  6. A record book, and the habit of using it weekly

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common expensive mistake on a water plant?

A dosing tank running empty without anyone noticing. The plant carries on with no antiscalant or dechlorination protection, and by the time anyone realises, the membranes are scaled or chlorine damaged.

Why should pre-treatment be sized on feed flow?

Because feed flow is higher than plant output. A seven hundred and fifty LPH plant at sixty five percent recovery has a feed flow of about eleven hundred and fifty LPH, and pre-treatment sized for the lower figure chokes immediately.

What is wrong with closing the reject valve when output falls?

It raises recovery, which makes output appear to recover, but it concentrates the reject stream further and accelerates the scaling that probably caused the drop. The plant then declines faster than before.

When should a membrane be cleaned?

At ten to fifteen percent decline from baseline, not when output has halved. Fouling caught early cleans off readily, whereas leaving it too long means the deposits become firmly attached and cleaning recovers only part of the performance.

What do most plant failures have in common?

They are monitoring failures rather than equipment failures. Something stopped working, nothing was measuring it, and nobody found out for weeks. The instruments that would have shown the problem early cost very little.
