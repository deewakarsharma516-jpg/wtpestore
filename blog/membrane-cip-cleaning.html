Membrane CIP cleaning — step by step procedure

Aqua Filtration System, Faridabad · 9+ years in water treatment · Updated September 2026

Short answer: CIP means Cleaning In Place — circulating a cleaning solution through the membranes without removing them. Use an acidic cleaner for scale, and an alkaline cleaner for organic and biological fouling. Clean early, when performance has dropped 10 to 15 percent, not when it has halved.

When to clean

The recognised triggers are any one of these against your baseline:

  • Permeate flow down 10 to 15 percent
  • Pressure drop across the housing up 15 percent
  • Salt passage up 5 to 10 percent (rejection falling)
Do not wait. Fouling caught early cleans off readily. Left until flow has halved, deposits become firmly attached and cleaning recovers only part of the performance — sometimes very little.

Which cleaner to use

Type of foulingSignsCleaner
Scale (calcium, carbonate)Flow down, pressure up, hard water feedAcidic cleaner
Organic / biologicalSlimy, pressure drop rising, often smellsAlkaline cleaner
Colloidal / siltPoor SDI feed, gradual declineAlkaline
Iron / metal oxidesReddish depositsAcidic
MixedUncertainAlkaline first, then acidic
When both are needed, always clean alkaline first. Acid applied over organic fouling can set the deposit and make it harder to remove.

What you need

  • CIP tank — big enough to hold the volume of the housings plus piping
  • CIP pump — able to give the required flow at low pressure
  • Cartridge filter in the CIP loop, to catch loosened debris
  • Cleaning chemicals — see our RO chemicals range</a> and <a href="/ro-chemicals.html">ZeroScale RO chemicals
  • pH meter — to monitor and adjust the solution
  • Permeate water for mixing and flushing — never raw water

The procedure

  1. Record the readings first — flow, pressure and TDS, so you can judge the result
  2. Flush with permeate at low pressure to displace feed water
  3. Mix the cleaning solution in the CIP tank using permeate, to the concentration on the product instructions
  4. Check and adjust the pH to the recommended range
  5. Circulate at low pressure — enough flow to scour, but low pressure so you are not forcing foulant into the membrane. Send the return to the CIP tank, not to drain
  6. Soak — stop the pump and let the solution stand for 30 to 60 minutes. This is where much of the work happens
  7. Circulate again for 30 to 60 minutes, monitoring pH. If the pH shifts significantly, the cleaner is being consumed and may need topping up
  8. Flush thoroughly with permeate until the pH and conductivity return to normal
  9. If a second stage is needed, repeat with the other cleaner and flush again between them
  10. Return to service, sending permeate to drain until quality is acceptable
  11. Record the readings again and compare

Important limits

ParameterGuidance
Cleaning pressureLow — just enough to circulate
Cleaning flowHigher than normal service flow
TemperatureWarm helps, but stay within the membrane limit
pH rangeWithin the membrane manufacturer's stated limits
Contact timeSoaking is more effective than pressure
Exceeding the pH or temperature limits will damage the membrane permanently. The limits are on the membrane datasheet and they are not advisory. If you do not have the datasheet, ask us before cleaning.

Safety

  • Wear gloves, eye protection and an apron
  • Add chemical to water, never water to chemical
  • Keep acid and alkali well apart and clearly labelled
  • Ensure ventilation
  • Have clean water available for washing
  • Never mix cleaners together

Judging the result

OutcomeMeaning
Flow and rejection largely restoredSuccessful — fouling removed
Flow improved, rejection unchangedPartial — some ageing present
Little changeEither wrong cleaner, or the membrane is finished
Rejection worse after cleaningPossible membrane damage — check pH used

If two properly executed cleanings produce no improvement, the membrane has reached end of life — see our replacement guide.

How often should CIP be needed?

On a well designed plant with correct pre-treatment and dosing, once or twice a year is normal. If you are cleaning every two or three months, the real problem is upstream:

  • Antiscalant dosing inadequate or interrupted
  • Pre-filtration insufficient, SDI too high
  • Recovery set too high
  • Chlorine or biological growth in the feed

Frequent cleaning is a symptom, not a solution. See our membrane life guide.

Records worth keeping

For each CIP note the date, the readings before and after, which cleaner was used, the concentration and pH, and the contact time. Over a couple of years this record tells you a great deal about how your plant is really performing.

Frequently asked questions

When should an RO membrane be CIP cleaned?

When permeate flow has fallen ten to fifteen percent from baseline, pressure drop has risen fifteen percent, or salt passage has increased five to ten percent. Cleaning early is far more effective than waiting until performance has halved.

Should I use an acidic or alkaline cleaner?

Acidic for scale and metal oxides, alkaline for organic and biological fouling. Where both are present, always clean alkaline first, because acid applied over organic fouling can set the deposit.

Why should cleaning be done at low pressure?

High pressure forces foulant further into the membrane rather than lifting it off. Cleaning relies on flow to scour the surface and on soaking time, not on pressure.

What happens if the pH limit is exceeded during cleaning?

The membrane can be permanently damaged. The pH and temperature limits are on the membrane datasheet and must be respected. If you do not have the datasheet, check before cleaning rather than after.

How often should CIP cleaning be needed?

Once or twice a year on a well designed plant. If you are cleaning every two or three months, the real problem is upstream — usually inadequate antiscalant dosing, poor pre-filtration or recovery set too high.
